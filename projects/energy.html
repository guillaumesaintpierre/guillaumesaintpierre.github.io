ENGINEERING NOTE 01

01 — The engineering question

A data centre consumes electricity continuously, and much of that energy is rejected as heat. Meanwhile district-heating networks need high-temperature energy, especially during cold periods. Connecting the two requires more than a yearly heat balance: the temperature levels, demand timing, electrical consumption and economic counterfactual must all be consistent.

I developed a reproducible hourly engineering model for a hypothetical 1 MW IT facility with a heat pump connected to a district-heating network. The objective was to screen technical feasibility, estimate operating value and identify sensitivities before any equipment design or investment decision.

ENGINEERING NOTE 02

02 — Physics and dispatch logic

Every hour, the model estimates recoverable IT heat, source-side temperature, network supply conditions and heat demand. A Carnot-based heating COP multiplied by an assumed 0.50 second-law efficiency represents heat-pump performance. The model then constrains thermal output by heat-pump capacity, source heat and simultaneous demand.

COPmodel = ηII × Tcond / (Tcond − Tevap) temperatures in kelvin

The hour-by-hour dispatch compares the avoided cost of gas-boiler heat with the additional cost of compressor electricity, pumping and variable operating expenses. The heat pump can turn off when recovery is not economically attractive. This prevents assigning value to heat that cannot be used or is more expensive to supply.

Useful output is checked using the two energy identities Qsource + Wcompressor = Qcondenser and Qcondenser = Qdelivered + Qlosses. The simulation rejects violations of these balances.

ENGINEERING NOTE 03

03 — Input data, transparency and reproducibility

The first run is an entirely synthetic, fully documented benchmark covering the 8,760 UTC hours of 2025. IT loading, weather, heating demand, source temperature, energy tariffs, installed capacity and costs are explicitly assumed.

I then built a second scenario with observed Energinet DK2 day-ahead power prices and DMI Kastrup temperature observations for 2025. The importer handles the October switch to quarter-hourly Danish power-market prices by assembling complete quarter-hour blocks into UTC hours. It also aligns DMI’s preceding-hour temperature observations to the correct interval.

The import workflow stores input provenance and source metadata. The Python code includes physics, tariff, dispatch and scenario tests. Observed prices and temperatures do not turn an assumed district-heating demand curve into real customer measurements.

ENGINEERING NOTE 04

04 — Results: reference against historical boundary conditions

The observed 2025 price and weather boundaries change the modelled building demand and heat-pump operating economics. In that run the system supplies 4.86 GWh/year useful heat, covering 75.2% of the modelled demand, with an operating COP of 4.06. The reference synthetic run supplies 5.05 GWh/year with COP 4.01.

ENGINEERING NOTE 05

05 — What drives performance?

I varied supply temperature, capture fraction and heat-pump efficiency independently. Under the chosen constraints, lowering network supply temperature improves COP and economic value, even though it need not maximize condenser-side MWh. A fixed source stream can yield less condenser heat when the compressor contributes less electrical work.

ENGINEERING NOTE 06

06 — Capacity sizing and business interpretation

An oversized heat pump adds capital cost without necessarily accessing additional source heat or heat demand. I tested condenser capacities from 0.40 to 2.00 MW on a discrete 0.05 MW grid, recalculating hourly dispatch and a hypothetical CAPEX curve at each point. The modelled economic optimum occurs near 0.90 MW under the synthetic benchmark and 0.85 MW with historical 2025 weather and price boundaries.

The 2025 case gives around €189k/year of conditional net avoided boiler operating cost, and €1.30m discounted 20-year NPV under assumed 6% real discounting, CAPEX, overhaul and fuel costs. The break-even avoided heat value is approximately €48.89/MWh. These are avoided system costs versus a modelled gas boiler — not heat-sales revenue, bankable cash flows or a plant valuation.

A conditional operational displacement estimate is about 906 tCO₂/year, before lifecycle effects and the site-specific electricity/heat baselines are established.

ENGINEERING NOTE 07

07 — Conclusions and what I learned

1 / Temporal matching matters

IT heat is continuous, district demand is strongly seasonal, and only coincident useful delivery has economic value.

2 / COP alone is insufficient

A better temperature lift reduces electricity input, but system value also depends on recoverable source heat and demand constraints.

3 / Size for value, not maximum output

Under the assumed cost curve, a smaller heat pump produces nearly the same useful heat and a stronger conditional NPV.

The next stage of research would be to replace assumed temperature and demand curves with measured site data; use manufacturer COP and part-load maps; include the true network connection costs, local tariffs, storage and realistic legal heat offtake arrangements; and obtain quotations before evaluating financial bankability. The study is deliberately a transparent technical screening exercise, not a financing recommendation.
